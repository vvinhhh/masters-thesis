“You Will Be Replaced!” AI in Record-to-Report

“You will be replaced!”

for the 1000th time. What real AI usage says about accounting work.

For twenty years, every new technology has arrived with the same headline: accountants are about to disappear. Generative AI is the latest, and accounting is consistently cited among the occupations most exposed to it. This story checks that claim against how people actually use Claude, task by task, across Record-to-Report: keeping the books, closing them, reporting, and planning what comes next.

The reversal

Rank the six parts twice: by how much is handed to AI, then by how much AI actually gets done. The order flips. Close & Consolidation and Record fall. Master Data and Analysis climb to the top.

Not how hard the task is, but how heavy

What drives success

How much expertise a task needs says almost nothing about whether Claude succeeds at it, on either platform. Load does. In the chat, each extra hour the task would take a person costs about 4 points of success. Through the API, the more context a task needs, the lower its success.

The gap is the work people still do

Back to the start

Blue is what AI could do. Red is what people hand to it at work. Green is what it successfully gets done. Where red reaches past green, in Close & Consolidation and Record, more is handed over than delivered. Where green reaches past red, people working with AI get done more than the automated use alone suggests.

The space between blue and green is the reviewing, reconciling and deciding that still runs through people, and it is where accounting jobs are being redrawn.

Could be doneHanded to AIDone successfully

Red counts automated work use at volume. Green counts every use, collaborative or not, weighted by success. That is why the two can cross.

Accountants aren't disappearing. Their work is moving.

AI isn't taking over accounting in one sweep. It takes parts of tasks, mostly in posting, closing and analysis, and those parts work unevenly. So people stay in the loop to direct the AI, check its output and correct it. The mix of tasks in an accountant's week is changing: more reviewing and verifying, more knowledge of the neighbouring parts of the process, and more of the judgment calls that models still get wrong.

The question is not only how much of Record-to-Report is exposed to AI, but what the location, composition and effectiveness of that exposure imply for how accounting work is reallocated between people and machines.

What this means for finance teams

Track success, not just use

Usage counts and task coverage overstate what AI contributes. Monitor how often AI output holds up, part by part of the process.

Keep review controls on AI entries

Automation in Record and Close is visible but not yet reliable. Verification becomes a task in its own right that roles can be designed around.

Match the channel to the task

High-volume, codifiable work suits API automation only where the context it needs can be bounded. Judgment-heavy analysis and planning gain more from AI directed by experienced staff.

Protect how juniors learn

The gains go to people who already hold the expertise to direct and check the AI, so the paths through which juniors build it matter more, not less.

These are early, directional findings. They rest on Claude data only, and a Claude model judges task success, not accountants. The method page covers what that means.

How it was measured

Everything behind the story, on one page

Where the data comes from, how the 91 tasks were built, what each measure means, and where the evidence is thin.

The short version

The Anthropic Economic Index publishes, in privacy-preserving aggregates, which O*NET occupational tasks people use Claude for, on the Claude.ai chat and through the API. This study keeps the 91 tasks that belong to Record-to-Report, gives each one a weight for how much working time it takes, an estimate of the expertise it needs and a wage, and follows them through five releases of the Index. Releases 4 and 5 add how the AI was used and how often it succeeded, which is what the effectiveness measures need.

From O*NET tasks to a panel

Step 1

Pick the tasks

O*NET 30.2 task statements of accounting and finance occupations: 212 task-occupation rows.

Step 2

Place them in R2R

Several language models and a human rater classify each task into one of six subprocesses, with a consensus round. 91 tasks are in scope.

Step 3

Weigh and rate them

Hours per week (three models, averaged), expertise in years (ridge regression on BLS education), wage (BLS OEWS), capability β (Eloundou et al.).

Step 4

Match real usage

Anthropic Economic Index Releases 1 to 5, Claude.ai and 1P API, merged into one long panel.

Step 5

Build the measures

Exposure, concentration, expertise composition and effective exposure, per task, subprocess and process.

How this page differs from the thesis text

  • Release 5 is shown throughout the story. The thesis reports Release 4 alongside it, and the explorer covers Releases 1 to 5.
  • All shares are shown as percentages. The thesis reports observed exposure as an index from 0 to 1, so 0.40 there is 40% here.
  • The usage gate is on (WorkUsage ≥ 100) for observed exposure, as in the thesis's main specification. Without the gate, process exposure is 35% instead of 20%.
  • The three bars in “Used is not the same as done” are all shares of working time, so they can be compared directly. The thesis states the first two as task counts: 90 of 91 tasks capable and 41 of 91 touched.
  • Effective and observed exposure measure different things. Effective exposure counts every use, collaborative or not. Observed exposure counts only work use at volume. That is why a part can score higher on the first than on the second.
  • “The busiest fifth takes 76%” is the top-20% share on the Claude.ai Lorenz curve (Figure 10). Gini coefficient: 0.71.

Where the evidence is thin

  • Almost every measure comes from AI annotation. Clio assigns conversations to tasks, Claude-based classifiers judge collaboration mode and success, and three models estimate task hours. Agreement between models shows reliability, not validity.
  • Task success is the least validated measure. A Claude model judges Claude conversations, without review by accountants.
  • Claude only. Claude users may not represent accounting professionals, and API traffic may come from developers building tools. Near-zero exposure in Report and Planning may reflect use of other platforms.
  • Usage, not outcomes. The Index records use. It cannot show what that use does to jobs, and the fixed O*NET task list cannot show new tasks appearing.
  • Two releases for the key measures. Observed exposure, concentration and success exist only for Releases 4 and 5.
