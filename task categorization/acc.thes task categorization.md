**Rationale, Overview, and Precendence**

*Rationale.* O\*NET organises occupational tasks by job role, not by process. A Financial Manager's task list spans activities that, within the R2R framework, belong to entirely different subprocesses, from journal-entry preparation through variance analysis to forward-looking budget planning. Therefore, aligning O\*NET task data with the R2R process structure is a prerequisite for the exposure analysis that follows. However, mapping these tasks to R2R subprocesses cannot be accomplished by keyword matching: a task description such as "Analyze financial data to ensure regulatory compliance" contains no subprocess-specific vocabulary, yet a domain-informed reader recognises it as an Analysis and Business Insight activity. What the classification requires is not lexical lookup but contextual reasoning that encompasses the capacity to read an abbreviated task description, activate knowledge of what that activity produces and for whom, and resolve it against a structured decision framework. This study responds to that requirement by adapting LLMs as annotators, using multiple language models as proxies for human judgement across the full O\*NET task corpus.

A growing body of evidence confirms that LLMs are effective annotation instruments, originally demonstrated in the context of data labelling for model training. Gilardi et al. (2023), Törnberg (2023), and Ziems et al. (2024) each independently found that LLMs match or exceed human annotators in accuracy, reliability, and inter-rater agreement across a range of classification tasks, from relevance and stance detection to political ideology and emotion classification in social media text. The consistent argument across these studies is that LLMs, as general models, encode sufficient contextual and domain background to reason about the meaning and intent behind a text rather than simply matching surface patterns, an ability characterises as traditionally considered uniquely human. This reasoning capacity is precisely what makes the approach transferable beyond its original training-data labelling context, where as Gilardi et al. (2023) and Ziems et al. (2024) conclude that LLMs are broadly suited for structured categorisation work that would otherwise require extensive human annotation or supervised training data. The O\*NET-to-R2R mapping needs fall squarely within this scope. Beyond accuracy, the method also offers scale and reproducibility relevant to this study. An LLM-based annotation pipeline is version-controlled and fully replicable by any researcher with API access, addressing a recognised limitation of human annotation studies where exact replication is rarely achievable. This study's resource constraints reinforce this consideration, where annotation at the required scale and with the required domain specificity would not have been feasible considering the scope of this thesis.

Ziems et al. (2024) also cautions against uncritical reliance on any single model, warning that LLMs exhibit high internal agreement even when inaccurate, meaning ensemble disagreement is not a reliable signal of error, and overconfident models left without human validation can distort research conclusions. Accordingly, this study integrates human classification as an independent rater alongside LLMs.

*Workflow.* The classification workflow proceeds in five sequential stages, each building on the outputs of the prior one. In the first stage, subprocess definitions and decision rules are constructed to serve as the shared codebook against which all raters will classify tasks. The human rater then completes all task classifications independently before any model outputs are produced to avoid anchoring bias that would arise from seeing model labels first. In the second stage, a validity run is conducted on a calibration sample, running multiple models across varying configurations of temperature, tier, and repeated runs. The purpose of such run is not to produce final classifications but to test the instrument robustness, interrogating whether variation in outputs reflects genuine task difficulty or systematic error introduced by model choice or parameter settings. The agreement and reliability statistics from this validity run then inform the appropriate configuration for the third stage, the production run, in which all 212 tasks are classified by each rater independently under the selected settings, with every rater required to provide not only a label but an explicit chain-of-thought reasoning for that label, with no visibility of other raters' outputs. In the fourth stage, any task that did not achieve unanimous agreement in the production round enters a consensus round where each rater is shown the anonymised reasoning of all other raters and is asked to produce a revised classification. Tasks reaching three-or-more agreement in the consensus round are resolved to that label, and any remaining two-to-two splits are escalated to a final human domain expert decision. The output of this pipeline is the validated R2R task register that serves as the input to Phase 2 exposure mapping.

*Precedence* for applying this method to occupational task classification in an economics context has already been established. Most closely related to this study's design is Autor and Thompson (2025), where GPT-4.1 was used to classify tasks from their dataset into an abstract/routine/manual taxonomy, achieving 89% agreement when model outputs were validated against a manual review of 100 tasks. Another instance concerns Eloundou et al. (2024), where GPT-4 was employed alongside human expert annotators to classify over 19,000 O\*NET tasks by AI exposure level, achieving strong cross-annotator consistency. Parshall and Lopez-Luzuriaga (2026) extended this logic into a multi-model consensus pipeline, using Claude, GPT, and Gemini to rate over 23,000 tasks along a three-axis taxonomy, reaching 75.0% pre-consensus pairwise agreement that improved to 90.8% post-consensus, with cross-validation against four independent ground-truth datasets confirming that their classifications track known external benchmarks. This result confirms both the viability of the multi-model approach and the validity of the intended instrument. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that LLMs classification of O\*NET task data is a reproducible and methodologically credible approach to occupational taxonomy work, and this study adapts their design to the domain-specific requirements of R2R subprocess mapping.

**Input Data**

O\*NET task data were drawn from Version 30.2 of the O\*NET Resource Center. The starting point for occupation selection was the Standard Occupational Classification system, with inclusion guided by the extended R2R definition adopted in this study, spanning both transactional and decision-support activities across the finance function. Four major SOC groups were identified as relevant: Management Occupations (11-0000), Business and Financial Operations Occupations (13-0000), Computer and Mathematical Occupations (15-0000), and Office and Administrative Support Occupations (43-0000). Within these, ten occupations were selected on the basis of their substantive coverage of R2R activities: Chief Executives (11-1011.00), Financial Managers (11-3031.00), Treasurers and Controllers (11-3031.01), Cost Estimators (13-1051.00), Accountants and Auditors (13-2011.00), Budget Analysts (13-2031.00), Tax Preparers (13-2082.00), Database Administrators (15-1242.00), Bookkeeping, Accounting, and Auditing Clerks (43-3031.00), and Billing and Posting Clerks (43-3021.00). From each occupation, only Tasks data are collected, specifically Task Statements, was extracted from O\*NET Resource Center, yielding a corpus of 212 tasks natural-language description for classification, alongside corresponding occupational title and task ID.

**Subprocess Definitions, Decision Rules, and System Prompt Design**

The classification labels were defined following the R2R definition reported in the literature review, combining both the bean-counting and business-partner objectives of a finance function to yield six subprocess definitions across Master Data Management, Record, Close & Consolidation, Report, Analysis & Business Insight, and Planning & Budgeting. An explicit OUT\_OF\_SCOPE category and an AMBIGUOUS holding label were added to the label set, producing eight allowed outputs in total.

Decision rules served two distinct functions. First, they acted as a scope filter. Since the occupations included extend beyond R2R activities, such as audit and assurance tasks, tax strategy, and general managerial coordination, a threshold rule was required to exclude tasks whose primary output is outside the process boundary. Second, since many O\*NET task descriptions are written at a level of abstraction that does not resolve to a single subprocess, the decision rules provide a classification hierarchy that prioritises the primary purpose and output of a task over its surface vocabulary, as well as secondary rules for boundary cases between adjacent subprocesses. The rules were designed to be structured enough to promote consistency while remaining open enough to not impose too rigid a schema that limits the models’ reasoning ability. 

The system prompt provided to each model followed the prompting guidelines established by Ziems et al. (2024): instructions were placed after context material, allowed labels were presented in a separated format, and models were required to produce chain-of-thought reasoning before committing to a final label. Responses were requested in JSON format to leverage model familiarity with structured output and to facilitate downstream parsing. Models were given eight allowed labels, which include the six subprocesses, AMBIGUOUS, and OUT\_OF\_SCOPE, and were required to select exactly one label to align with the single-assignment structure used by the Anthropic Economic Index data in Phase 2\. No worked examples were provided; the instrument relies on zero-shot reasoning against the defined constructs. 

The full subprocess definitions, decision rules, and system prompt are provided in Appendix . Based on these, the human annotator also completed classifications independently before seeing any model output.

**Validity Run**

The validity run was designed to evaluate instrument robustness before committing to a production configuration, by testing classification consistency across multiple dimensions of variation, including runs, temperatures, model tiers, and providers, on a stratified subset of the full corpus.

A 30% stratified sample of 64 tasks reflecting subprocess distribution of the human classification was drawn from the full 212 task corpus. Six models were included across 2 tiers of 3 providers, Claude Sonnet and Claude Opus (Anthropic), GPT-mini and GPT-flagship (OpenAI), and Gemini Flash and Gemini Pro (Google), run at both temperature 0.2 and temperature 1.0, with two runs per configuration. This produced 24 LLMs labels in total for each task that goes through the validity run. 

The agreement analysis was conducted on multiple levels. At the overall process level, agreement and reliability were evaluated across runs, temperature settings, model tiers, providers, and individual models, culminating in a full cross-LLM agreement statistic pooling all 24 rater-slots. A second set of tests examining each model's agreement against the human classification, as well as subprocess-level consensus strength and full-agreement rates,  coupled with a confusion matrix showing where disagreements concentrate across subprocess boundaries. Reliability statistics were computed as Krippendorff's nominal alpha, which was chosen over ordinal alpha because of the nominal structure of the classification task under consideration, as well as the fact that subprocess categories are not uniformly spaced on any underlying dimension i.e. the distance between Record and Close & Consolidation isn't the same empirical unit as the distance between Close & Consolidation and Report. Moreover, the confusion matrix mentioned above can directly surface where disagreements concentrate across specific subprocess boundaries in a more analytically tractable manner than baking a distance assumption into the reliability statistic itself. Supplementary statistics include mean pairwise agreement, full agreement rate across all raters, and Pearson correlation of task volume within each subprocess against the human distribution. Tendency to assign AMBIGUOUS or OUT\_OF\_SCOPE labels was also tracked to provide additional diagnosis for disagreement analysis.

*Results.* The full 24-rater instrument returns Krippendorff's α \= 0.739 (95% CI \[0.644, 0.818\]), with mean pairwise agreement of 0.842 and full agreement on 60.9% of tasks. The task-level mean consensus strength of 0.88 indicates that for the large majority of tasks, raters agree on a single label, and the task with the minimum consensus strength of 0.32 represents a genuinely boundary task where the description cannot be cleanly resolved with the decision rules, demonstrating a data-inherent limitation rather than an instrument failure. Intra-model stability at temperature 0.2 is near-perfect, with every model returning α ≥ 0.90 across repeated runs, against a range of 0.785 to 0.908 at temperature 1.0. This establishes temperature 0.2 as the appropriate setting for production, where a single run at that temperature provides essentially the same information as a repeated run. Mid-tier models return higher internal consistency (α \= 0.797) than flagship models (α \= 0.757) and outperform them on human agreement across all three providers. The explanation lies in the punt-rate, as flagship models systematically assign OUT\_OF\_SCOPE at rates 7 to 14 percentage points above the human baseline (65 to 72% versus the human's 57.8%), while that for mid-tier models cluster closely around human calibration (53.1 to 62.5%). Such conservative bias thus depresses flagship agreement with the human annotator, with Claude Opus recording the lowest human alignment of any model at 70.3% and an α of 0.538. Pearson correlations between each model's subprocess volume profile and the human profile range from 0.988 to 0.995, confirming that all models share the human's structural understanding of how many tasks belong to each subprocess, even when individual assignments diverge.

Disagreement analysis reveals that the dominant source of inter-rater variation is at the R2R scope boundary, referring to tasks classified as a substantive subprocess by some raters and OUT\_OF\_SCOPE by others, rather than within-R2R subprocess confusion. Classification errors concentrate at the perimeter of the process, not within its internal structure. Where disagreement does occur, it is structurally explainable rather than indicative of instrument failure. The Master Data Management leakage to OUT\_OF\_SCOPE reflects the broad occupational scope of Database Administrators (15-1242.00), whose task list extends well beyond financial systems into general infrastructure management. The Record to OUT\_OF\_SCOPE leakage captures tasks that encompass both recording activities and preceding clerical or physical actions whose primary output is not yet a journal entry or ledger record, but directly support record entry work. The Report to OUT\_OF\_SCOPE edge case refers to tasks involving consulting legal documentation before the preparation of reports, which Decision Rule 0 excludes due to its preparatory nature preceding actual reporting work. 

Where raters agree a task is in-scope, they agree on the subprocess at high rates. The two principal within-R2R disagreement concerns Analysis & Business Insights versus Planning & Budgeting and Record versus Close & Consolidation, which reflects genuine task-description ambiguity between adjacent subprocesses. These tasks carry vocabulary from both subprocesses without specifying a primary purpose or output that decision rules can cleanly resolve them. Nevertheless, misclassification between these would not distort the exposure analysis in Phase 2 the way misclassification across the cognitive spectrum of the process would. Accordingly, the validity run reveals no systematic instrument failure, and the production run shall proceed with a single run at temperature 0.2 using mid-tier models from all three providers. 

**Production Run**

The full 212-task corpus was classified under the production configuration, where each rater produced a classification label and explicit reasoning for every task, with no visibility of other raters' outputs. The human classification from the initial labelling exercise was carried forward as the fourth rater. Due to its configuration, the agreement analysis for the production run retains only the tests that remain meaningful, including overall cross LLM-agreement, model pairwise agreement, agreement with human rater, consensus strength, disagreement using a confusion matrix, punt rates, and volume correlations.

*Results.* The three-model production instrument returns Krippendorff's α \= 0.739 (95% CI \[0.674, 0.795\]) with mean pairwise agreement of 0.835 and full agreement on 76.4% of tasks. Since the α is essentially identical, the stability of α relative to the validity run confirms that the instrument's reliability did not degrade when scaled from the 64-task calibration set to the full corpus. The higher full agreement rate (76.4% vs. 60.9%) is also simply an effect of fewer raters rather than improved classification quality.

Raw agreement with the human annotator declines modestly for all three models relative to validity-run performance by approximately 0.07 to 0.10 α units, which is expected, as the production corpus spans a broader range of occupational codes and task types, surfacing genuinely harder classification cases that the smaller calibration set did not fully represent. The subprocess-level consensus profile is similarly modest in its deterioration, but the overall task-level mean consensus strength remains 0.87, consistent with validity-run levels.

Of the 212 tasks, 131 achieved unanimous 4/4 agreement and were auto-resolved directly into the final register. The structural pattern of disagreement is largely preserved from the validity run. The dominant axis remains substantive subprocess versus OUT\_OF\_SCOPE, now across 127 tasks, with that category still retaining a consensus strength of 0.906 and full agreement of 72.4%. Two within-R2R confusion axes emerge more prominently at the larger task scale. The Record versus Close & Consolidation disagreement (\*) reflects a systematic divergence on where the transaction-to-general-ledger posting boundary sits, representing cases where task description genuinely split between two adjacent subprocesses. The Analysis & Business Insight versus Planning & Budgeting disagreement (\*) remains consistent with the validity run, where tasks whose descriptions carry keywords from multiple subprocesses prompted raters to weigh different elements of the description as primary. Accordingly, this property of O\*NET task descriptions that are written to describe what is done rather than why it is done produce rater divergence when purpose must be inferred. This represents a pattern that the following consensus round is specifically designed to resolve.

**Consensus round**

Each rater, including three models and the human, was provided with the reasoning, but not the labels, of all other raters from the initial production round, presented in anonymised and randomised order. AMBIGUOUS was removed from the allowed label set for this round, requiring each rater to commit to a definitive subprocess assignment or OUT\_OF\_SCOPE. Models were instructed to re-examine the task fresh against the decision rules and others’ reasoning. The human annotator completed consensus classifications first and independently before reviewing model outputs. Majority rule was then applied across the four consensus-round raters, where tasks with ≥ 3/4 agreement were resolved to the majority label, and tasks with a 2/2 split were flagged for subsequent human review.

*Results.* Of the 81 disputed tasks, 73 resolved through majority rule: 34 reached full consensus (all four raters converging after reading each other's reasoning), and 39 resolved as three-to-one majority. Eight tasks remained as 2/2 splits and required explicit human judgment. The reconciliation round produces a substantial improvement in cross-model agreement. Krippendorff's α rises from 0.739 pre-consensus to 0.875 post-consensus (95% CI \[0.824, 0.919\]), and the full agreement rate increases from 76.4% to 88.7%. Mean pairwise agreement improves from 0.835 to 0.925, and the full agreement rate rises from 76.4% to 88.7%. The improvement is approximately symmetrical across all three models, indicating that the reconciliation process genuinely revised classifications through shared reasoning rather than propagating the most confident model's prior label.

The per-subprocess disagreement profile in the residual cases maps cleanly onto the structural sources identified across both prior runs. Three patterns account for the majority of residual disagreement. The dominant pattern is three-model consensus on OUT\_OF\_SCOPE against a lone human in-scope assignment. In these cases, the models apply Decision Rule 0 more conservatively than the human, either because the task description contains non-R2R domain vocabulary or because the description operates at a level of abstraction that does not specify a financial output. These are cases where the human's domain knowledge enables a contextualised reading that the task description alone does not support. A smaller but analytically important set shows three-rater consensus on one in-scope subprocess against another assignment to an adjacent one. For example Task 21531 *Develop, maintain, or analyze budgets, preparing periodic reports that compare budgeted costs to actual costs* receives human, Claude, and Gemini assignments to Planning & Budgeting against an GPT assignment to Analysis & Business Insight, which is a disagreement that reflects genuine boundary ambiguity between two adjacent subprocesses. The eight 2/2 split tasks are those whose description simultaneously spans multiple subprocesses, and no single decision rule produces a clean resolution for either type.

Within those that passed through consensus, there were four overrides made by human annotator, which clustered into four documentable patterns of systematic model weakness. These include domain prior contamination, where surface vocabulary activated non-financial reasoning, as in a cost-estimation task flagged as construction-related; audit carve-out misapplication, where verification tasks were incorrectly treated as assurance rather than preparer-side data quality control; vague-purpose tasks enabling richer human inference task descriptions written at a level of abstraction that prompted a literal model reading and a contextually richer human reading; and coordination-level task ambiguity, with management tasks whose R2R relevance is implicit rather than stated. These four override patterns are not idiosyncratic human preferences but documentable systematic model weaknesses arising from the interaction between O\*NET task description brevity and LLM literal interpretation, advocating/supporting/aligning with advocation of Ziems et al. (2024) for inclusion of human annotators alongside LLMs. 

**Final Task Register**

The final task register contains 91 tasks classified as in-scope across the six R2R subprocesses, with a further 121 assigned OUT\_OF\_SCOPE (see Table X). Record is the most populated subprocess (31 tasks), followed by Planning and Budgeting (22 tasks), Report (13 tasks), Analysis and Business Insight (12 tasks), Master Data Management (8 tasks), and Close and Consolidation (5 tasks). Of the 92 in-scope tasks, 39 were resolved automatically through unanimous agreement in the production round, and 52 were resolved through the consensus round, either by three-or-more majority (45 tasks) or final human expert review (8 tasks). The register is structurally robust for Phase 2 exposure mapping.
